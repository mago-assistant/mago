# Mago Privacy Mode: legal research (issue #97)

Verbatim module copy of the research document of 10-11 September 2026 (originally a shared
Google Doc linked from issue #97), kept in-repo so the legal grounding of privacy mode is
versioned next to the code it justifies. Part 1 is the Dutch summary, part 2 the full English
research with sources and adversarial verification. This is research by the project, not legal
advice to merchants.

## Part 1: GDPR and Dutch Law Research (English)

Prepared 10 September 2026 for mago-assistant/mago issue #97 (Privacy Mode). All factual claims in this document were verified against primary sources (EDPB, Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens, EUR-Lex, curia.europa.eu, the dataprivacyframework.gov registry, and the providers’ own legal documentation) in an adversarial fact-check on the same date. Corrections from that check are incorporated in the text; the verification summary is in section 11.
### 1. Context and roles under the GDPR
Mago is an open source Magento 2 admin assistant module. Merchants install it themselves, bring their own LLM API key, and run it on their own infrastructure. This shapes the legal analysis in a fundamental way: the module maintainers never touch personal data and are not a controller or processor for any merchant’s customers. The maintainer is only a producer of software in the sense of recital 78 GDPR.
The roles in a deployed installation are: the merchant is the controller of its customer data, and the LLM provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, Azure OpenAI) is the merchant’s processor for whatever the module sends to the API. The merchant therefore needs, for any personal data that reaches the LLM: a signed data processing agreement covering the clauses of art. 28(3) GDPR (OpenAI and Anthropic both offer standard DPAs), a legal basis (typically art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interest, with a documented balancing test), an updated art. 30 record of processing and privacy notice naming the provider, and adherence to data minimisation (art. 5(1)(c)) and purpose limitation (art. 5(1)(b)).
Because the module is open source and the maintainer holds no contracts, there is no way for the maintainer to “paper” a data flow on the merchant’s behalf. Platform vendors such as Zendesk, Klaviyo and Shopify send customer data to LLM sub-processors under their own negotiated DPA chains; that route does not exist here. Every compliance obligation lands on the merchant, and the only instrument the module itself has is what it does by default.
The EDPB ChatGPT taskforce report (May 2024) is directly relevant to the design: it stresses that compliance responsibility cannot be shifted to end users and explicitly recommends filtering and anonymising personal data before it reaches the model.
### 2. Privacy by design and by default (art. 25)
Art. 25(2) GDPR requires that, by default, only personal data necessary for each specific purpose is processed, and EDPB Guidelines 4/2019 (v2.0) spell this out across volume, retention, accessibility and disclosure to third parties: the protective state must be the default and must not require action to obtain.
One nuance matters for an open source module and was confirmed in verification: art. 25 formally binds the controller (the merchant), not the software producer. Paragraph 1 of the guidelines states that processors and producers are not directly addressed by art. 25; recital 78 only encourages them. The practical force is nevertheless the same, through two provisions aimed at the controller:
* Paragraph 44: a controller using third party or off-the-shelf software “should perform a risk assessment of the product and make sure that functions that do not have a legal basis or are not compatible with the intended purposes of processing are switched off”.
* Paragraph 94: controllers should choose systems with built-in data protection, which is what makes privacy-protective products deployable at all.
The consequence: a module that sends customer personal data to an LLM by default is a module that a compliant EU merchant may not deploy as-is. Since an open source project cannot verify that each merchant signed a DPA, chose an EU deployment, or updated their privacy notice, shipping the privacy-protective state as the only state is the design that makes every default installation compliant.
### 3. Third-country transfers and provider positions
The EU-US Data Privacy Framework currently stands: the EU General Court dismissed the Latombe annulment action on 3 September 2025 (case T-553/23, Curia press release 106/25). Latombe appealed to the CJEU on 31 October 2025 (case C-703/25 P, EUR-Lex CELEX 62025CN0703); the appeal is pending as of September 2026. Prudent design does not rely on the DPF surviving.
For the two most likely providers the DPF is in fact irrelevant, as registry verification showed:
* OpenAI is not in the dataprivacyframework.gov registry. EEA customers contract with OpenAI Ireland Ltd and transfers rest on Standard Contractual Clauses per OpenAI’s own DPA. Default API retention is up to 30 days for abuse monitoring. European data residency for API projects exists (announced February 2025, endpoint eu.api.openai.com, per-project at creation) but requires prior approval for modified abuse monitoring plus a Modified Retention amendment; it is not plain self-service.
* Anthropic is also not in the DPF registry (neither active nor inactive; a widely circulated claim that Anthropic is DPF-certified is false). Its DPA incorporates the 2021 SCCs (modules 2 and 3, with UK and Swiss addenda). The first-party Claude API offers no EU processing region at all: the platform documentation’s inference_geo parameter accepts only “us” and “global”, and data is stored in the US. EU-region processing of Claude models is available only via AWS Bedrock EU regions (Stockholm and Ireland in-region, plus EU cross-region inference profiles) or Google Vertex AI EU endpoints. Zero data retention is a per-organisation enterprise agreement, with a carve-out for safety classifier results.
* Azure OpenAI offers the cleanest processor story for EU merchants: EU Data Zone deployments process prompts and responses within the Azure EU Data Boundary under the standard Microsoft DPA, with no training on customer data. Two verified nuances: data at rest stays in the resource’s designated Azure geography for all deployment types (EU storage therefore comes from creating the resource in an EU region, not from the Data Zone SKU), and the EU Data Boundary can include EFTA countries such as Norway and Switzerland, so it is strictly “EU Data Boundary” rather than “the EU”.
Conclusion: EU-region, retention-limited processor setups suitable for a proper art. 28 chain exist at all three providers, but none of them is what a merchant gets with a plain API key. A hypothetical “send customer data” toggle flipped without such a setup would in most cases result in a US transfer under SCCs with 30-day provider-side retention, on infrastructure the merchant never assessed.
### 4. Dutch specifics
The Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens published a warning on 6 August 2024 (“Caution: use of AI chatbot may lead to data breaches”) reporting actual breach notifications: a GP practice employee entered patient medical data into an AI chatbot, and a telecom employee entered a file of customer addresses. The AP’s position is that entering personal data into a chatbot provider’s systems contrary to internal agreements constitutes unauthorised access and therefore a reportable data breach. It instructs organisations to make explicit agreements about what may be entered, to block or secure chatbot use, and to arrange with providers that data is not stored. Follow-up warnings appeared after the Eindhoven municipality leak. The framing matters for Mago: customer data into a third-party LLM without a proper processor arrangement is, in the AP’s baseline view, a datalek.
The AP and ACM have also jointly stated that customer-service chatbots must not deceive users into thinking they are human; this is only marginally relevant since Mago serves the merchant’s admin, not consumers.
EDPB Opinion 28/2024 (December 2024) on AI models shows the board’s scepticism toward “the model makes it anonymous” arguments: models trained on personal data are not automatically anonymous, and anonymity requires identification or extraction via queries to be very unlikely, assessed case by case.
The UAVG adds nothing LLM-specific, with one hard particular: the BSN may only be processed with a statutory basis, so BSN-like values must never leave the shop under any mode.
### 5. EU AI Act
An admin shop assistant is not high-risk (no Annex III category). The relevant obligation is Article 50 transparency, applicable from 2 August 2026: natural persons interacting directly with an AI system must be informed they are interacting with AI, unless this is obvious to a reasonably well-informed person. Fines for transparency violations reach EUR 15 million or 3 percent of worldwide turnover. A verified detail: the grace period until 2 December 2026 (introduced via the Digital Omnibus) applies only to the art. 50(2) marking and detection duty for AI-generated content of systems already on the market before 2 August 2026; the art. 50(1) inform-humans duty has no grace period.
By integrating a general-purpose AI model into a product distributed under its own name, the Mago project is likely a downstream provider for AI Act purposes, and the merchant a deployer. The practical impact is small: a clearly visible “AI assistant” label in the admin UI and documentation covers the obligation. The “obvious from context” carve-out arguably applies to a chat panel named as an AI assistant, but an explicit label is cheap insurance.
### 6. Where the personal-data line runs
Three legal anchors draw the line:
* Pseudonymised data remains personal data. Recital 26 GDPR, restated in EDPB Guidelines 01/2025 on pseudonymisation: data that can be re-attributed using separately kept information remains personal data, and all obligations (legal basis, DPA, transfer rules) continue to apply. Replacing an email address with a token before sending to the LLM does not take the flow out of the GDPR, because the shop keeps the mapping. What it does achieve: it is a genuine art. 25/32 safeguard, it strengthens a legitimate-interest balancing, and it drastically reduces transfer risk because the provider receives data it cannot attribute. (Status note: these guidelines were adopted in January 2025 as the version for public consultation and no final version exists as of September 2026; recital 26 is the hard anchor, the guidelines are supporting guidance.)
* Identifiability is broad. CJEU Breyer (C-582/14): data is personal when identification is possible with means reasonably likely to be used, including information held elsewhere. An order record stripped of name and email but carrying the order increment ID is still personal data, because the ID links straight back to the customer in the shop database. Postcode plus date plus basket contents can single a person out on their own.
* Genuine aggregates are non-personal. The WP29 anonymisation test (Opinion 05/2014): no singling out, no linkability, no inference. Revenue per day, order counts, average order value, top-selling SKUs and similar statistics pass, provided cell sizes are adequate; an “aggregate” over one customer’s orders is that customer’s personal data.
Applied to the module’s data categories:
Never to the LLM: customer names, email addresses, phone numbers, street addresses, IP addresses, review nicknames; BSN-like values and payment data under any mode.
Personal via linkability, send only tokenised: order increment IDs, customer entity IDs, postcode-date-basket combinations, per-customer order histories, review free text (user-authored, can embed anything).
Freely allowed: adequate-cell-size aggregates (revenue, order counts, AOV, top products, review statistics, customer counts), catalog and product data, stock, CMS content, store configuration, URL rewrites, cron and cache and indexer state, Magento documentation.
Residual risks to document rather than solve in code: the admin pasting personal data into the chat box (this is exactly what the AP’s breach cases were), and the admin user’s own name as employee personal data.
### 7. Hard-wired versus configuration setting
Nothing in the GDPR forbids a controller from sending customer data to a processor under a proper art. 28 and Chapter V chain, so a legally clean opt-in is theoretically possible. Four considerations nevertheless favour hard-wiring privacy mode as the only mode:
1. Art. 25(2) and EDPB 4/2019: the default must be the minimal-disclosure state, and controllers are told to switch off functions they have no legal basis for. A default-on data flow fails this immediately; even an opt-in toggle only works if the merchant who flips it actually has the DPA, the transfer mechanism and the updated notices in place.
2. The AP treats personal data entered into a third-party chatbot without arrangements as a reportable breach. A toggle that a merchant can flip in thirty seconds, without any check that a provider DPA exists, manufactures exactly those breaches.
3. Transfer fragility: the DPF is under CJEU appeal and neither OpenAI nor Anthropic relies on it anyway. A product whose compliance story requires no customer data to cross the Atlantic is future-proof against the next Schrems moment.
4. The open source position: platform vendors that do offer customer-data AI features (Zendesk, Klaviyo, Shopify) are themselves the processor, with negotiated sub-processor terms, disclosed sub-processor lists and objection mechanisms. An open source module whose merchants bring their own API keys has no equivalent instrument. It cannot verify a DPA, cannot force an EU deployment, and cannot accept responsibility contractually. The default is the only lever, which argues for the strictest default in the market rather than parity with the platform players.
If commercial pressure ever demands a “full data” mode, it should be a separate, later decision with hard gates: off by default, ACL-protected, requiring explicit confirmations that a provider DPA is signed, the privacy notice and art. 30 record are updated, and an EU-region or zero-retention deployment is configured, with the acceptance logged. That is not part of the current scope.
### 8. Masking versus not sending
The implementation question “mask the data or do not send it at all” resolves to: do not send by default; mask (tokenise) only where the conversation needs a stable handle.
1. Masking buys less legal ground than it appears to: tokenised data with a server-side mapping is pseudonymisation and remains personal data (recital 26, EDPB 01/2025). Not sending wins outright: absent data cannot leak, cannot be retained by the provider, and needs no explanation in a DPA conversation.
2. Masking is a much larger build: a token map per conversation, rehydration in the chat panel and in every answer widget, and consistency across edge paths (usage log, debug log, streamed chunks). Every missed path is a leak. Stripping is one filter at the existing choke point.
3. Free text cannot be masked reliably: review content can embed names, emails and phone numbers in any form; regex masking there is false assurance. For user-authored text, not sending is the only honest option (v1: the assistant can count and route reviews, not summarise them).
4. The usability loss of not sending is small: the admin almost always supplies the order number or email address in the question itself (which no technical measure covers anyway), tool results carry names mostly as display convenience, and the admin-panel deep link (attached UI-side, outside the LLM payload) still leads to the full record. Aggregates, statuses, totals and dates remain untouched.
Where masking does earn its place: bare entity ids in list results (top_spenders, recent_signups) where the assistant must be able to refer to a row across turns; [customer_1] style tokens are cheap there and need no rehydration. Summary rule: strip the four PII-carrying tools, tokenise bare ids only, never mask free text.
### 9. Implications for the design
1. Ship privacy mode as the only mode. Minimise tool results server-side, strip or tokenise direct identifiers before the API call, pass aggregates freely.
2. Document it honestly: the claim is “no directly identifying customer data ever leaves the shop”, not “the GDPR does not apply”. Tokenised data is pseudonymised personal data; merchants still need their provider DPA for whatever residual personal data flows (including anything admins type into the chat).
3. Treat order IDs, postcodes and per-customer histories as personal data and tokenise them; keep aggregates, catalog, CMS and configuration data unrestricted.
4. Do not rely on the DPF in documentation; describe SCCs and EU-region options (OpenAI EU residency projects, AWS Bedrock or Vertex AI EU regions for Claude, Azure EU Data Zone) as the transfer mechanisms merchants should prefer.
5. Add a visible “AI assistant” disclosure in the admin UI before 2 August 2026.
6. Never send BSN-like values or payment data; document admin-pasted free text as a residual risk with a warning in the UI or docs.
### 10. Open items (apart from anonymizing skills)
1. AI Act label is already due. Art. 50(1) (inform natural persons they interact with AI) applies since 2 August 2026 with no grace period; only the art. 50(2) content-marking duty has the December 2026 grace. Mago currently relies on the “obvious from context” carve-out; the explicit admin-UI label is one line of work and should ship regardless of privacy mode. File as its own issue.
2. Three security findings to file as separate issues: the config reader’s fail-open denylist (SMTP usernames, client ids and trans_email addresses reach the provider today), admin deep links in tool results embedding the admin URL secret key, and the stored conversation history that is never pruned (retention and right-to-erasure gap).
3. Implementation spec issue to be opened once the team agrees on the no-toggle decision and the boundary in this document.
4. Pending branch risk: the upstream feature branch that opens admin forms from chat would give the assistant access to arbitrary admin screens, including customer forms, invalidating the “only four PII-carrying tools” conclusion. Privacy mode must land before or together with that branch, or that branch must adopt the field classification from day one.
### 11. Verification summary
An adversarial fact-check on 10 September 2026 verified the claims above against primary sources. Confirmed directly: OpenAI’s absence from the DPF registry and SCC basis; OpenAI EU residency mechanics and 30-day default retention; Anthropic’s EU-via-Bedrock/Vertex situation and enterprise zero-retention; Azure Data Zone behaviour; the Latombe timeline (T-553/23 dismissed 3 September 2025, appeal C-703/25 P pending); the AI Act art. 50 dates including the limited 2 December 2026 grace; and the AP breach warnings. Corrected during verification: Anthropic is not DPF-certified (a circulating secondary-source claim); art. 25 binds the controller rather than the software producer (the design conclusion survives via paragraphs 44 and 94 of EDPB 4/2019); and EDPB Guidelines 01/2025 remain in consultation-version form.

## Part 1: Samenvatting (Nederlands)

De korte versie voor niet-technische lezers. De volledige onderbouwing en bronnen staan in deel 1 (Engels).
### Wat is privacy mode

Mago is een AI-assistent in het Magento-beheer: de beheerder stelt vragen, de assistent haalt gegevens uit de shop op en een externe AI-dienst (zoals OpenAI of Anthropic) formuleert het antwoord. Zonder maatregelen kunnen daarbij klantgegevens (namen, e-mailadressen, bestellingen) bij die externe AI-dienst terechtkomen.
Privacy mode zorgt ervoor dat dit niet gebeurt: direct herleidbare klantgegevens verlaten de shop niet. De assistent werkt dan met geanonimiseerde verwijzingen en met totaalcijfers, en blijft daarmee volledig bruikbaar voor omzetvragen, productinformatie, configuratie en vrijwel alle dagelijkse taken. Alleen bij het opzoeken van een individuele klant of bestelling ziet de AI-dienst een neutrale verwijzing in plaats van de echte naam; de beheerder ziet in het scherm gewoon de echte gegevens.
### Waarom dit standaard aan staat, zonder uitknop

1. De AVG (artikel 25) vereist dat software standaard zo min mogelijk persoonsgegevens verwerkt en deelt. De privacyvriendelijke stand hoort de fabrieksinstelling te zijn, niet iets dat een winkelier zelf moet aanzetten.
2. De Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens heeft meermaals gewaarschuwd dat klantgegevens invoeren in een AI-chatdienst zonder goede verwerkersafspraken een meldplichtig datalek is. Een instelling die dat met één klik mogelijk maakt, organiseert precies dat risico.
3. Mago is open source: winkeliers gebruiken hun eigen sleutel bij de AI-dienst. De makers van Mago kunnen dus geen contracten of garanties voor hen regelen, zoals grote platforms dat wel doen. De standaardinstelling is daarmee het enige echte beschermingsmiddel dat het product heeft.
### Wat wel en niet naar de AI-dienst gaat

Wel: totaalcijfers (omzet, aantallen bestellingen, best verkopende producten), productinformatie, pagina-inhoud, instellingen en Magento-documentatie.
Niet: namen, e-mailadressen, telefoonnummers, adressen, reviewnamen en reviewteksten, en nummers die naar één klant of bestelling herleidbaar zijn. Die worden vervangen door neutrale verwijzingen voordat er iets naar de AI-dienst gaat.
### Wat dit voor de winkelier betekent

Privacy mode maakt Mago standaard privacyvriendelijk, maar vervangt de eigen verplichtingen van de winkelier niet. Wie een AI-dienst gebruikt sluit daar zelf een verwerkersovereenkomst mee af en vermeldt de dienst in de eigen privacyverklaring. Ook geldt: wat een beheerder zelf in het chatvenster typt valt buiten elke technische bescherming, dus het blijft verstandig om geen klantgegevens in de vraag zelf te zetten. De assistent is bovendien duidelijk herkenbaar als AI, zoals de Europese AI-verordening vanaf augustus 2026 vraagt.
### Maskeren of niet sturen

Voor de uitvoering is gekozen voor niet sturen als uitgangspunt, niet voor maskeren. Gemaskeerde gegevens met een terugvertaaltabel blijven juridisch gewoon persoonsgegevens, terwijl weggelaten gegevens per definitie niet kunnen lekken. Maskeren vraagt bovendien veel extra techniek die op elke plek sluitend moet zijn, en op vrije tekst (zoals reviewteksten) is het principieel onbetrouwbaar. Het verlies aan gebruiksgemak is klein: de beheerder noemt zelf al het ordernummer of e-mailadres in de vraag, en de link naar het volledige klantrecord in het beheer blijft gewoon werken. Alleen kale volgnummers in lijstjes krijgen een neutrale verwijzing, zodat de assistent er in het gesprek naar kan blijven verwijzen.
### Openstaande punten

1. Het “AI assistent”-label in het beheerscherm is wettelijk al verplicht (AI-verordening, sinds 2 augustus 2026, geen overgangstermijn voor deze plicht). Klein werk, apart op te pakken.
2. Drie losse beveiligingsbevindingen uit hetzelfde onderzoek moeten als eigen tickets gefiled worden: de configuratie-lezer laat vandaag al inloggegevens en contactadressen door, beheerlinks in antwoorden bevatten een geheime beheersleutel, en de opgeslagen chatgeschiedenis wordt nooit opgeschoond (bewaartermijn en recht op vergetelheid).
3. Zodra het team akkoord is, moet er een implementatie-issue komen met de technische specificatie.
4. Er staat een grote wijziging klaar (admin-formulieren openen vanuit de chat) die de assistent toegang tot veel meer schermen geeft, ook met klantgegevens. Privacy mode moet vóór of samen met die wijziging landen.
### Conclusie

Privacy mode wordt de enige stand van Mago, niet een instelling. Direct herleidbare klantgegevens verlaten de shop nooit richting de AI-dienst; de assistent blijft daarbij volledig bruikbaar omdat totaalcijfers en productdata vrij beschikbaar blijven en individuele gegevens alleen als neutrale verwijzing reizen. Dit maakt elke standaardinstallatie van Mago verdedigbaar onder de AVG en toekomstbestendig bij strengere Europese regels, terwijl de winkelier alleen nog de eigen papieren verplichtingen (verwerkersovereenkomst, privacyverklaring) hoeft te regelen.
